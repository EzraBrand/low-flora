### ‎4. Rhus coriaria L. [=Linnaeus] Sumac.

‎Tanner's sumac — widespread throughout the whole Mediterranean region — is called in Mishnaic Hebrew אוג Flora [=Löw, Die Flora der Juden] 3, 219. The word is also used in modern Eretz-Yisrael Hebrew (Hasade [=Hasade, Hebrew agricultural monthly] 3, 233. 4, 391). It is a tree (Pea [=Mishnah Peah] 1, 5. Sifra [=Sifra, midrash on Leviticus] 87<sup>b</sup>), whose ripening berries turn reddish (Maas. [=tractate Maʿaserot] 1, 2). On this basis Jastrow [=Jastrow, Dictionary of the Targum, the Talmud etc.] wrongly emends האיבין ארומים, LA. [=variant reading] האובים, האיבום (ms. [=manuscript] in Zuckermandel, Errata [=Zuckermandel, Tosefta, errata]) T. [=Tosefta, ed. Zuckermandel] Maas. r. [=tractate Maʿaser rishon] 1, 81<sub>18</sub> to האוגין, for it is the bh. [=Biblical Hebrew] אב (Schwarz, Tosifta [=Schwarz, Tosefta] 1, 317).

‎The tree was less esteemed in Judaea than in the rest of Eretz Yisrael (Dem. [=tractate Demai] 1, 1), where it grows everywhere today. Luncz [=Kaftor waferaḥ, ed. Luncz] asserts that it grows especially in the region of Hebron (Kaftor [=Estori ha-Farḥi, Kaftor waferaḥ] 482). On the Carmel it is rarer (ZDPV. [=Zeitschrift des Deutschen Palästina-Vereins] 30, 134), yet a ruin there is called <i>chirbet summāḳa</i> (a. O. [=at the place cited] 158). The leaves still serve today for the preparation of tanbark (H. Fischer, Wirtschaftsgeogr. von Syrien [=Fischer, Wirtschaftsgeographie von Syrien] 74).
