### ‎2. Pistacia Lentiscus L. [=Linnaeus] Mastic shrub.*)

‎A small evergreen tree or shrub. As a component of evergreen maquis it is certainly endemic throughout the entire Mediterranean region from Syria and Palestine to the Canary Islands, and also in North Africa (Hehn [=Victor Hehn, Kulturpflanzen und Haustiere] 429). Illustration in Vietz [=Ferdinand Bernhard Vietz, Icones plantarum medico-oeconomico-technologicarum] 2, 11. Vietz in 1804 still praises the medicinal effects of mastic resin, whereas it is now officinal only in Austria (Strassburger <sup>15</sup> [=Eduard Strasburger et al., Lehrbuch der Botanik, 15th ed.] 577). Bloch [=Samson Bloch, Sĕbīlē ʿōlam] 2, 82, probably the first among Jews, describes the tree and its resin. Modern Palestinian Hebrew: האלה קשת העלים = سريس [sarīs] (Hasade [=Hasade, Hebrew agricultural monthly] 1, 3, 32). Inaccurately בטנים**) (3, 454. 4, 312), erroneously שיח הלוט (6, 206).

‎The tree also yields mastic (Haynald, Mézgák [=Lajos Haynald, A szentírási mézgák és gyanták termőnövényei] 14. 28) in Palestine (P. [=George E. Post, Flora of Syria, Palestine and Sinai] 207), but for commerce it has for a long time (Benjamin v. [=of] Tudela, p. [=page] 20 of the Grünhutschen Übersetzung [=L. Grünhut, Die Reisebeschreibungen des R. Benjamin von Tudela] translation) been obtained only on the island of Chios, from a cultivated variety (Moeller <sup>2</sup> [=Joseph Moeller, Lehrbuch der Pharmakognosie, 2nd ed.] 431). The annual production averages 125,000 kg [=kilograms]. Josua Benveniste [=Joshua Benveniste, Śĕde Jĕhōšuaʿ], rabbi in Constantinople, reports in his commentary on jḤalla [=Jerusalem Talmud, tractate Ḥalla] 4, 9 f. [=folio] 177<sup>d</sup>, published in 1662, that in the Turkish Empire mastic might be produced only on the island of Chios (כיאו). Already Benjamin of Tudela can report about the island of Chios (כיא) only that 400 Jews live there and that the tree from which mastic מצטיקי is gathered is found there (1, 25 Asher. [=A. Asher, The Itinerary of Rabbi Benjamin of Tudela]).

‎In the Middle Ages mastic was regarded as a remedy; today it is used for varnishes and putties. In the Orient it has been in use as a chewing resin since ancient times, and the Palestinian kind was considered the best according to the testimony of Plinius [=Pliny the Elder, Naturalis historia] (Killermann [=Sebastian Killermann, Die Blumen des heiligen Landes] 1, 34). Plin. 14, 25 says: in oriente optimam tenuissimamque [resinam] terebinthi fundunt, dein lentisci, quam et masticen vocant.
