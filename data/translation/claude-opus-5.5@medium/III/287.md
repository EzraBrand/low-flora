A similar Mishnaic report is referred to the same scene (Derenbourg, Essai [=Derenbourg, Essai sur l'histoire et la géographie de la Palestine] 98; Schürer<sup>2</sup> [=Schürer, Geschichte des jüdischen Volkes, 2nd ed.] 1, 224; Grätz, hebr. [=Graetz, Geschichte, Hebrew translation by Rabinowitz] 1, 448). Some centuries later the scene repeats itself: the inhabitants of Maḥuza pelt R. [=Rabbi] Zeïra, who makes a remark displeasing to them, with their citrons (Kidd. [=Kiddushin] 73<sup>a</sup>; Derenbourg a. O. [=at the place cited]).

The reference of the fruit of the goodly tree to the <i>eṯrōg</i> is undisputed tradition within rabbinic Judaism (Maim. [=Maimonides], Introduction to the Mischnakomment. [=Mishnah commentary] f. [=folio] 54<sup>a</sup> Romm [=Romm edition (Vilna)]; Maim. H. Lulab [=Hilkhot Lulav] 7, 2; L. Löw gS. [=Leopold Löw, Gesammelte Schriften] 1, 280). The necessity of a Sinaitic tradition is actually proven by the fact that the identification of the <i>eṯrōg</i> with the fruit required in the Bible cannot otherwise be substantiated (Leon da Modena, Bechinaṯ hadaṯ [=Leon da Modena, Beḥinat ha-Dat] 26). This is still emphasized — in accordance with the traditional view — by Chajim Sofer, rabbi of the orth. [=Orthodox] community in Budapest (Kōl Sofer [=Chajim Sofer, Kol Sofer], 1882, note on Abot [=Pirqe Abot] 3, 1). Maimūnī [=Maimonides (commentary on the Mishnah)] attempted a rational justification: the <i>eṯrōg</i> is suitable because it stays fresh for the entire duration of the festival, which cannot be said of peaches, pomegranates, quinces or pears (More [=Moreh Nevukhim] 3, 43 end). In point of fact this hardly holds true for pomegranates and quinces.

In Talmudic times the philological conscience sought aggadic supports for the equation <i>hādār</i> = <i>eṯrōg</i>; later, etymological justifications were sought for it. Nachmānī [=Nachmanides, Commentary on the Pentateuch] (Emor [=Parashat Emor] 106<sup>a</sup>; Meiri, Magen Abot [=Menahem ha-Meiri, Magen Avot] 111 end; Rapoport, Erech Millin [=Rapoport, Erekh Millin] 253) attempts, ingeniously but incorrectly, to extract from <i>eṯrōg</i> a root רגג that is supposed to correspond to hebr. [=Hebrew] הדר. For him the <i>eṯrōg</i> is the paradise fruit that arouses desire — רגג. It led Eve into sin; therefore, together with other plants, it is to serve for atonement. As late as 1819 M. J. Landau [=Moses Israel Landau, editor of the Aruch] (Ar. [=Aruch] 1, 231) tries to derive etymologically "to gild", hence golden fruit, and Kohut [=Aruch ed. A. Kohut] claims (1, 336; 8, 274) that Persian ترنك [turunk] is synonymous with <i>hādār</i> (?) and wishes to find in this the justification of the traditional identification.

In the 13th century Eleazar of Worms calculated that the numerical value of פרי עץ הדר, 659, is the same as that of the word [א]תרוגים (Rokeach [=Sefer ha-Roqeaḥ by Eleazar of Worms, Zolkiew edition] 39<sup>b</sup> Zolkiew).
