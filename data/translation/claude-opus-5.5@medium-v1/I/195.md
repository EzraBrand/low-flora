### ‎2. Pistacia Lentiscus L. Mastic shrub.*)

‎A small evergreen tree or shrub. As a component of evergreen maquis it is certainly endemic throughout the entire Mediterranean region from Syria and Palestine to the Canary Islands, and also in North Africa (Hehn 429). Illustration in Vietz 2, 11. Vietz in 1804 still praises the medicinal effects of mastic resin, whereas it is now officinal only in Austria (Strassburger <sup>15</sup> 577). Bloch 2, 82, probably the first among Jews, describes the tree and its resin. Modern Palestinian Hebrew: האלה קשת העלים [hāʾēlāh qəšat heʿālîm] = سريس [sarīs] (Hasade 1, 3, 32). Imprecisely בטנים [bāṭnîm]**) (3, 454. 4, 312), erroneously שיח הלוט [śîaḥ hallôṭ] (6, 206).

‎The tree also yields mastic (Haynald, Mézgák 14. 28) in Palestine (P. 207), but for commerce it has for a long time (Benjamin of Tudela, p. 20 of Grünhut's translation) been obtained only on the island of Chios, from a cultivated variety (Moeller <sup>2</sup> 431). Annual production averages 125,000 kg. Joshua Benveniste, rabbi in Constantinople, reports in his commentary on jḤalla IV 9 f. 177<sup>d</sup>, published in 1662, that in the Turkish empire mastic may be produced only on the island of Chios (כיאו [kyʾw]). Already Benjamin of Tudela has nothing more to report about the island of Chios (כיא [kyʾ]) than that 400 Jews live there and that the tree from which mastic, מצטיקי [mṣṭyqy], is gathered is found there (1, 25 Asher.).

‎In the Middle Ages mastic was regarded as a remedy; today it is used for varnishes and putties. In the Orient it has been in use as a chewing resin since ancient times, and according to the testimony of Pliny the Palestinian was considered the best (Killermann 1, 34). Plin.
