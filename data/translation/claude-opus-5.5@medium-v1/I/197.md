In Arabic, P. Lent. is also called ضرو [ḍirw]. Nuwairī, in E. Wiedem. XLIX 21, says that <i>kamkām</i> is the lentisk resin of the tree <i>ḍirw</i>, or according to others its leaves or bark. It flows out elastic, black as pitch. The tree resembles the terebinth, <i>buṭm</i>, or according to others a very large oak, but is said to be smoother and more delicate. It bears clusters of fruit, like the <i>buṭm</i>. Used for toothpicks: Silberberg, Dināwarī 78.

The cited Midrash renders the biblical <i>lōṭ</i> as mastic: מסטוכי [msṭwky] (var. lect. מסטכי [msṭky], מסתיכי [mstyky], corrupt מפטדי [mpṭdy], correctly RDK. s.v. <i>lōṭ</i>: מוצטכא [mwṣṭkʾ]) and adds: עבידא כול [ʿbydʾ kwl] (variant readings according to Albeck's*) communication: עבידה בול [ʿbydh bwl], יעבידא כיל [yʿbydʾ kyl], עבירא כול [ʿbyrʾ kwl], עביד הביל [ʿbyd hbyl]), which is probably meant to signify “in little lumps.” Menaḥem b. Solomon (1139) says: mustichi is like frankincense and consists of small grains like grains of salt.

In the Tannaitic period mastic was chewed in Palestine, either as a remedy or — as still today (Luncz on Farḥi p. 662) — to improve the smell of the mouth — εὐωδία [euōdia] Diosk. I 93 Spr. I 67 W. ZDMG. 28, 702 Kautchuk Persan <i>ḳenderan</i>. Orientals like to produce increased salivation by chewing resinous substances, such as mastic and pistachio resin (<i>sakkis</i>). What is used is <i>ḳenderan</i>, a rubber-like mass made from the thickened latex of several species of Scorzonera and Podospermum.
